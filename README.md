## Running the examples

The six examples were tested with base MATLAB R2023a Update 5 on 64-bit Windows 11 Pro, version 25H2. No special hardware is required.

With MATLAB already installed, the examples require no separate installation. Download the folder, including `inputs/`, and open it as the current folder in MATLAB. Each line below runs independently, opens one figure and returns its numerical values in a structure. Pass `false` to compute without plotting, for example `wave = demo_phase_wave(false)`.

On the test machine, the two particle trajectories together took about 14 seconds. The reference-distribution calculation took about 24 seconds, and each of the other four examples ran in under a second after MATLAB had started.

```matlab
traj  = demo_common_noise();
async = demo_asynchronous_order();
wave  = demo_phase_wave();
pot   = demo_probability_potential();
mfpt  = demo_first_passage();
ref   = demo_reference_distribution();
```

## Model and parameters

Each particle has a position angle $x_i$ on the ring and an internal phase $\theta_i$. The amplitudes $R_+$ and $R_-$ measure how tightly the sums and differences of these angles cluster within the same population of $N$ particles,

$$
R_\pm=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp\!\left[\mathrm{i}(x_i\pm\theta_i)\right]\right|.
$$

$J_1$ and $K_1$ are the pairwise coupling strengths in position and phase. $J_2$ and $K_2$ are their three-body counterparts. Each example uses Lorentzian half-width $\Delta=1$ for both the natural velocities and frequencies. In a channel comparison, both deterministic interactions remain present. Noise acts through one channel at a time.

| Example | $(J_1,J_2,K_1,K_2)$ | Noise intensity used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single realizations | $(2,5,7,9)$ | $D_1=10$ or $D_2=10$ |
| Asynchronous order | $(7,8,-1,9)$ | $0.01\le D\le 1$ for the means, $D=1$ for the density |
| Phase-wave fluctuations | $(1,5,6.5,9)$ | $D=10$ for variance rates. The coefficients themselves depend only on $R$ under the phase-wave reduction. |
| Probability potential | $(1,5,6.5,9)$ | $D=1$ |
| First passage | $(1,5,6.5,9)$ | $0.01\le D\le100$ |
| Reference distribution | $(7,8,1.5,9)$ | $D=1$ |

## Reading the outputs

**Particle trajectories.** The supplied input contains the initial $x_i$, $\theta_i$, natural velocities and natural frequencies of the 10,000 simulated particles. Each run integrates Eq. (1) from $t=0$ to 120 by stochastic Heun with step 0.01 and records both amplitudes every 0.1. A position-noise increment and an independent phase-noise increment are shared by all particles at each step. The seed 2027102610 reproduces the noise history of the selected realization in each channel. The output shows the full trajectories, including the initial transient. In MATLAB R2023a all 1,201 recorded values of each amplitude in each channel agree with the archived trajectories.

**Fluctuations near the asynchronous state.** The left panel shows the theoretical mean of $(R_++R_-)/2$ with $N=10{,}000$. The right panel gives the density for either branch at $D=1$. At this intensity the predicted mean is 0.0222144 for pairwise noise and 0.0177245 for higher-order noise. The higher-order curve stays horizontal to the order retained in the small-amplitude theory.

**Phase-wave response.** For the continuum phase wave with $R_+=0$, the Ott–Antonsen reduction gives $a_1(R)=R(1-R^2)/2$ and $a_2(R)=R^3(1-R^2)/2$ for the ordered branch $R=R_-$. At a fixed initial $R$, the short-time variance of its increment grows at rate $D a_\ell^2(R)$ in channel $\ell$. At $R=0.5$, the higher-order rate is one sixteenth of the pairwise rate. The example also checks both coefficients by integrating over the phase-wave angle distribution at this amplitude.

**Local probability potential.** Equation (10) compares the local zero-current density at $R$ with that at the unstable noise-free amplitude $R_u\approx0.312$, so $U_\ell(R_u)=0$. A lower potential means a higher local reference density. At $D=1$, the difference between the local maximum and minimum is approximately 0.161 for pairwise noise and 7.41 for higher-order noise. These comparisons use relative density on the interior of the amplitude interval.

**Downward first passage.** Equation (11) starts the ordered branch at its stable noise-free amplitude $R_H\approx0.606$ and stops the clock at its first arrival at $\varepsilon=0.01$. The solution remains finite towards $R=1$. At $D=1$ the predicted times are about 45.2 for pairwise noise and 150,750 for higher-order noise. The figure enlarges the range around the observation time of 1,000. The output field `log10MFPT` contains the base-10 logarithms for all 21 intensities, including values above the plotting limits.

**Short-time response at equal branch amplitudes.** For each prescribed $R_+=R_-=R$, the code constructs a reference angular distribution for locked and drifting particles using the same velocity and frequency pairs on both branches. With $Q=R_+^2+R_-^2$, $G_\ell(Q)$ is the initial rate of change in mean $Q$ caused by channel $\ell$, relative to noise-free evolution from the same distribution and divided by $D$. This example evaluates Eq. (15) at 101 amplitudes between 0.05 and 0.95 using the 10,000-node Lorentzian quadrature. Interpolating between adjacent points gives zero crossings at 0.549 and 0.699. Between them, pairwise noise lowers short-time mean order while higher-order noise raises it. On this subset of the manuscript grid, the recomputed curves agree with the archived theory values to within $5\times10^{-5}$ in absolute response.
